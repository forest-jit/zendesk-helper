# Editing patterns

Use this guide for systematic reviews and ambiguous edits. A phrase alone rarely justifies an edit: judge what it contributes to the passage and whether it fits the writer's voice. The first five patterns deserve early attention when their rhetorical scaffolding adds no information.

## Staging instead of stating

### 1. Artificial contrasts

Look for “not X but Y,” “not just,” “not merely,” reversed “rather than” constructions, contrasts split across sentences, and clipped negative tails. Remove a negative half that rejects something no reader has claimed. Keep contrasts that correct a real misconception or convey two distinct facts.

Before: “It's not just about the beat; it's part of the song's aggression and atmosphere.”

After: “The beat contributes to the song's aggression and atmosphere.”

### 2. Repeated closers and dramatic fragments

Cut one-line paragraphs that restate the preceding paragraph, explanations of what an example already demonstrates, and repeated “That is the real win” endings. Keep short sentences that add a consequence or fact. Merge fragments when they obscure the claim; preserve intentional rhythm.

Before: “Caching cuts repeat work. That is the real win.”

After: “Caching cuts repeat work.”

### 3. Sayings that sound deep

“The real question,” “at its core,” “the heart of the matter,” and “the architecture of” can dress an ordinary point as a revelation. State the specific claim when the metaphor adds nothing.

Before: “At its core, what really matters is whether teams can adapt.”

After: “What matters is whether teams can adapt.”

### 4. Staged run-ups

Remove routine announcements such as “Let's dive in,” “Here's what you need to know,” and standalone performances of candor such as “Honestly?” Keep conversational words when they naturally belong to the writer's sentence.

Before: “Let's break this down. The cache expires after ten minutes.”

After: “The cache expires after ten minutes.”

### 5. Arguing with no one

“I’m not saying,” “Don't get me wrong,” and imagined bad alternatives can be leftovers from drafting. Keep objections the audience actually raised and alternatives it would realistically weigh. Retain any substantive information when removing the defense.

## Rhythm by rule

### 6. Forced triads

Three items are fine when all three matter. Merge interchangeable abstractions or repeated examples rather than deleting distinct information merely to avoid a list of three.

Before: “The event has talks, panels, and networking. Expect innovation, inspiration, and insights.”

After: “The event has talks and panels, with time for networking.”

### 7. Repeated sentence openings

Merge or vary several accidental repetitions of the same subject or opening. Preserve deliberate anaphora and useful parallel structure.

Before: “She noted the door. She noted its lock. She filed both away.”

After: “She noted the door and its lock, then filed both away.”

### 8. Dashes as the universal connector

Choose punctuation that makes the relationship between clauses clear. Follow the voice-sample exception and semantic protections in SKILL.md. A single dash is not evidence of poor writing.

### 9. Stacked qualifiers

Simplify “could potentially possibly” without increasing certainty. Keep genuine uncertainty, scope statements, and necessary notices. “Perhaps,” “tends to,” and explicit inference labels can be appropriate.

Before: “The policy could potentially have some effect on outcomes.”

After: “The policy may affect outcomes.”

### 10. Mechanical compound hyphenation

Check compound modifiers in context: “a well-documented process” often becomes “the process is well documented.” Retain fixed hyphenated terms and follow the target style. Do not apply a universal before/after-noun rule to every compound.

### 11. Passive voice and missing subjects

Use an active subject when it clarifies responsibility and the actor is known. Passive voice is useful when the actor is unknown or irrelevant. Do not invent an actor to avoid it.

## Inflation and borrowed authority

### 12. Stock vocabulary

Clusters of “delve,” “tapestry,” “pivotal,” “meticulously,” “landscape,” “showcase,” and “underscore” can make prose generic. Replace them when a plain, accurate word fits. Keep literal meanings, technical uses, and the writer's characteristic vocabulary; words are not inherently AI-generated.

### 13. Inflated significance

Check “stands as a testament,” “plays a key role,” “indelible mark,” stock challenges-and-outlook sections, and promises of a bright future. Keep demonstrated significance and concrete plans. Remove significance asserted only by the wording.

Before: “The institute was established in 1989, marking a pivotal moment in its history.”

After: “The institute was established in 1989.”

### 14. Vague connections

Replace “associated with” or “linked to” with the specific relationship when it is given. Preserve vague wording when the relationship is unknown rather than guessing.

Before: “She is associated with the orchestra, which she founded and conducts.”

After: “She founded and conducts the orchestra.”

### 15. Shallow participial phrases

Check appended “highlighting,” “reflecting,” “symbolizing,” “ensuring,” and “fostering” clauses. Keep them when they convey supported facts or relationships. Remove unsupported interpretations without substituting a new interpretation.

### 16. Sales language

In neutral prose, replace “nestled,” “breathtaking,” “must-visit,” “renowned,” and similar promotion with concrete descriptions. In marketing copy, preserve the persuasive purpose while making claims specific and supportable.

### 17. Borrowed authority

“Experts believe” and prestige outlet lists can substitute for actual evidence. Preserve supplied attribution and citations. Name a source and its claim only when that information is available. Flag an unsupported substantive claim rather than inventing an expert, quote, date, or statistic. A follower count or publication credit may be a relevant fact, not decoration.

### 18. Avoiding simple verbs

Prefer “is,” “are,” or “has” when “serves as,” “stands as,” “boasts,” or “features” adds no meaning. Keep verbs that distinguish a role, service, or action.

Before: “The gallery serves as the association's exhibition space.”

After: “The gallery is the association's exhibition space.”

## Formatting by rule

### 19. Decorative bold labels

Remove excessive bolding and empty labels. Convert lists to prose only when scanning and meaning improve. Preserve definitions, acronym expansions, and distinct claims that a shorter version would lose.

### 20. Decorative headings

Prefer descriptive headings, usually in sentence case, over slogans or repeated titles. Remove decorative emoji, arrows, or separators where inappropriate for the target format. Preserve meaningful hierarchy and the user's style requirements.

### 21. Curly quotation marks

Match the writer or target format. Curly quotes are common editor output and are not a reason to rewrite prose. Preserve quoted words exactly.

## Chat and drafting leftovers

### 22. Chatbot residue

Remove “Certainly!”, “Great question!”, “I hope this helps,” and follow-up offers from prose intended to stand alone. Preserve greetings and sign-offs that belong to actual correspondence.

### 23. Knowledge disclaimers and guesses

Remove irrelevant model-training commentary. Do not strip meaningful “as of” dates or uncertainty. Omit an unsupported guess or explicitly flag the missing information when it matters. Lack of information about someone's childhood does not establish a private personality or a likely upbringing.

### 24. Headings repeated in opening sentences

Delete an opener that merely repeats its heading. Keep an opening sentence that supplies the claim the heading only names.

### 25. Writing about the document

Remove unnecessary accounts of how a document was assembled, narration of visible layout, and references to replaced drafts. Keep useful methodology, source credits, change logs, migration details, and caveats that affect the reader's decisions. Do not replace method narration with new facts.

## Writing for the reader

### 26. Re-explaining shared context

When conversation context is visible, put the answer or decision first. Remove background the recipient already supplied. Keep new reasoning, material limitations, and links needed for action. Detailed evidence may belong in the accompanying ticket or document, but do not drop it from a standalone report whose purpose is to record that evidence.

Before, in a thread that already established the problem: “The service is shared across accounts. Fixing it requires testing every account's merge flow. I'd prefer a separate ticket.”

After: “I'd open a separate ticket: the service is shared across accounts, so the fix requires testing every account's merge flow.”

## Limits

Preserve meaningful oddities, mixed feelings, dated references, genuine asides, and explainable first-person choices. Do not infer authorship from these patterns or use a publication-date cutoff as an authorship test. An edit succeeds when it preserves the writer's meaning and improves the text for its actual reader.
